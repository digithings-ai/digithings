---
title: "The architecture in brief"
summary: "One hub, many verticals, shared libraries and a single chat surface, held together by three principles."
---

At the centre is one service, the hub. Everything a user can do enters through
it, and everything the system can do on the user's behalf is decided by it. The
hub does not implement the capabilities itself. It delegates them to services
that each own one domain, which the book calls verticals. One of them retrieves
information. Another handles quantitative research. A third manages the system's
knowledge base. Each is independently useful and independently replaceable.

Around those sit libraries rather than services. The model access layer, the
tracing layer, the shared HTTP and audit helpers and the web-fetching engine are
all code you import, not processes you deploy. That distinction sounds pedantic
until you try to find a port for something that has never listened on one.

In front of all of it sits a single chat surface. It is the only way in for a
person. It authenticates the session and then talks to the hub, and it does not
call a vertical directly — so every capability is reachable only through one
audited path, and the surface stays thin no matter how many verticals exist.

Three principles explain nearly every design choice that follows. The system is
protocol-first, meaning capabilities are exposed through an open standard rather
than a private interface. Its structure is federated, meaning the hub
orchestrates while the verticals keep their own pipelines and tools rather than
being dissolved into it. And it is private by default, meaning a component
listens only where it is needed and exposes nothing to the network unless
someone deliberately opens it.

> **Why digithings**
>
> The stack is open source under a permissive licence, and it is built from
> modules you can take, change or drop. Nothing in the architecture requires you
> to buy anything to use it, and nothing binds you to one vendor once you are
> running it.
