Typing is not always how people think. A thought often arrives spoken and
half-formed, while walking or with the hands busy. digivoice catches it:
capture speech, transcribe it, reshape the transcript for where it is going
and speak results back. The whole loop runs on your own machine, and the
audio never leaves it.

The common alternative is to send audio to a hosted service, which trades
away the most personal stream a person produces: their voice and everything
in the room behind it. digivoice starts from the observation that the machine
in front of you is already fast enough. Running locally makes the loop
private because there is nowhere else for the audio to go, and it keeps
working offline.

## How it works

The loop has four stations. Capture records from the microphone to a local
audio file. A local speech model turns it into text. An optional rewrite pass
reshapes the transcript for its destination, a message, a note or a command,
using a small local model. Synthesis reads results back in a local voice.

```mermaid the loop completes on the machine; only the finished text travels further
flowchart LR
  MIC[speak] --> TRN[transcribe]
  TRN --> REW[rewrite]
  REW --> OUT[speak back]
```

The recording stays on disk as audio and every take lands in the history, so
a rewrite can be thrown away without losing what was said, and a failed
rewrite falls back to the raw transcript. History is an append-only log on
the machine that you can search and revisit. Settings can be read and
changed from the command line, so other tools can drive the loop, and a
built-in checkup reports plainly what the host can and cannot do.

## Where it fits

digivoice barely touches the rest of the stack. It never calls
the hub in the middle of a loop. When it is done, it pastes ordinary text into whatever is in focus: the chat, a research
brief, a note.

It has one home, the machine with the microphone. There is no voice service
to deploy, scale or pay for, because the voice was never hosted in the first
place.
